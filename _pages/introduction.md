The space of the Caribbean has been understood by scholars as marked by “its fragmentation; its instability; its reciprocal isolation; its uprootedness; its cultural heterogeneity; its lack of historiography and historical continuity; its contingency and impermanence; its syncretism” ([Benítez-Rojo 1](https://meredithsauer.github.io/caribbean-collages/bibliography/#works-cited)). This is the region where European colonists first established the plantation system, where African slaves were forced to labor over 100 years before the first slaves where brought to what is now the United States ([National Humanities Center](https://meredithsauer.github.io/caribbean-collages/bibliography/#works-cited)). The Caribbean “received more African slaves over a longer period of time, produced more sugar, and developed the most regimented plantation system of any region in the Americas” ([Morgan 10](https://meredithsauer.github.io/caribbean-collages/bibliography/#works-cited)). This prolonged period of encounter had an enormous human cost and resulted in profound cultural hybridization on the islands. Additionally, it is difficult to separate this process of cultural creolization from biological and ecological creolization. A kind of “creole ecology” resulted from the “constant arrival, dispersal, and mingling of new plants and animals” ([Morgan 11](https://meredithsauer.github.io/caribbean-collages/bibliography/#works-cited)).

Despite the impact of slavery and the plantation system on the Caribbean landscape, postcolonial studies and ecocriticism as critical lenses through which to read the literature of the region have historically been at odds, with postcolonial studies thought of as inherently anthropocentric. Postcolonialism is about hybridity; ecocriticism is about purity. Postcolonialism is concerned with displacement; ecocriticism is rooted in a sense of place ([Rob Nixon, qtd. in James 9-10](https://meredithsauer.github.io/caribbean-collages/bibliography/#works-cited)).

*Caribbean Literature and the Environment: Between Nature and Culture*, edited by Elizabeth DeLoughrey, Renee Gosson, and George Handley (2005) is one of the first full-length studies of the role of the environment in Caribbean literature.[^1]  In their introduction, they write of the importance of examining cultural and ecological creolization as interrelated processes:

>“This exchange \[of plants, animals, humans, and disease between the Caribbean and Europe\] was hardly mutually beneficial or even equitable. But emphasizing the transatlantic circuits of creolization destabilizes a presumed European purity and stability. In the Caribbean context, the discourse of creolization not only has served to emphasize the inevitable fragmentation of racial memory in the region, but it has helped to conflate human and natural histories, a welcomed shift that warns against the pretension that human societies can act independently of ecosystems.” (19)

This assertion is the starting point for my investigation of representations of the environment in Caribbean literature. I have selected three novels by Caribbean authors—Jean Rhys’s *Voyage in the Dark* (1934), Michelle Cliff’s *No Telephone to Heaven* (1987), and Jamaica Kincaid’s *Lucy* (1990)—that are set between the Anglophone Caribbean and Europe or the United States. Each of the three female protagonists is displaced from her home island and grapples with her identity in a foreign environment, though from different racial perspectives. Anna Morgan of *Voyage in the Dark* resents “being white and \[…\] all the things you get – old and sad and everything” ([Rhys 58](https://meredithsauer.github.io/caribbean-collages/bibliography/#works-cited)). Clare Savage of *No Telephone to Heaven* is mixed race, and Lucy is understood to be a person of color as well, when she states that “I was not a Briton and \[…\] until not too long ago I would have been a slave” ([Kincaid 135](https://meredithsauer.github.io/caribbean-collages/bibliography/#works-cited)). These novels are also deeply interested in the environment, the differences between a tropical and a temperate ecosystem and the way the home environment is replicated in memory and grafted onto a foreign environment.

Paying close attention to the way that the Caribbean landscape is written in these novels, the way that characters remember it and dream it and the way it is placed into relation with a disparate, unfamiliar European or North American landscape reveals a conception of land that rejects colonial ideas of land as territory ripe for possession, domination, and cultivation, and the idea that there is only one history of conquest.

[^1]: Other work on the subject that has followed includes Erin James’s *The Storyworld Accord: Econarratology and Postcolonial Narratives* (2015), Isabel Hoving’s *Writing the Earth Darkly: Globalization, Ecocriticism, and Desire* (2017), and Mary Ann Gosser Esquilín’s *Culture, Nature, and the Other in Caribbean Literature: An Ecocritical Approach* (2023).
